Astrophysics and Cosmology  

Mapping hydrogen -- the most abundant element in the Universe -- across the earliest times allows access to a tremendously rich cosmological and astrophysical dataset, one that will allow the most precise constraints thus far on theories of fundamental physics. The epoch of Cosmic Dawn, when the first stars and galaxies were born, is often seen as the 'final frontier' of observational cosmology today, yet it now lies within reach of upcoming ground- and space-based telescopes. Investigating this era offers valuable clues towards the physics of the normal matter (atoms and molecules) in the Universe and the mechanisms that shaped the formation of the first stars and galaxies.

Intensity mapping (a technique in which large cosmological volumes can be mapped without the need to resolve individual objects) is a promising complementary tool to extract valuable physics and astrophysics from cosmological observations. I developed a novel, data-driven framework which utilises our current astrophysical knowledge to interpret future intensity mapping data of cosmological hydrogen. It readily enables synergies with other line intensity mapping interpretations and more traditional galaxy observations. Extending this approach allows insights into the properties of the earliest black holes, combining multi-messenger observations from the James Webb Space Telescope and gravitational waves from Pulsar Timing Arrays (PTAs).

The current universe (the last 6 billion years of cosmic time) is characterized by the domination of the cosmological constant, which leads to the late-time accelerating phase of the universe. The extremely small value of the cosmological constant is a major puzzle in current cosmology (known as the cosmological constant problem). Resolving the cosmological constant problem is one of the important challenges of present-day cosmology.

Geophysics, Palaeontology and Ecosystems  

Earth science is deeply interdisciplinary, relying on approaches from physics, chemistry, biology and more recently, ecology and environmental science. At the current juncture, it is crucial to combine insights from diverse datasets, such as carbon dioxide and oxygen proxies, temperature records, and ecosystem populations to understand how the Earth system operates and evolves. Connecting evidence across widely separated intervals of geological time offers powerful insights that can help us confront contemporary challenges, such as the climate crisis.

I gave talks on Insights from data-driven techniques: from the cosmos to pre-historic earth at the Majorana-Raychaudhuri seminar on 22 August 2025, and at the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research (TIFR), Hyderabad, India on 23 September 2025.

I gave a talk on Lessons for the Earth's deadliest extinction for today's climate crisis at the Pint of Science Festival, Pune, India on 19 May 2025, and at the special science day lectures organized by the IAIAM on 3 March 2026.

I initiated the interdisciplinary workshop on 'Lessons and Challenges in Geophysical Data Analysis' at Fergusson College, Pune, India over 23-27 June 2025. This was the first interdisciplinary workshop conducted under the Geophysics theme all over India.
