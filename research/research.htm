Research Program  

We live in an era where the world's challenges — like climate change, public health crises, energy sustainability, cyber security, machine learning and artificial intelligence — are not confined to the boundaries of a single discipline. Solving these complex problems requires a collaborative lens, pooling together knowledge, tools, and perspectives from diverse fields. This is where interdisciplinary research becomes not only relevant, but essential.

Having always enjoyed a diverse profile of research interests in physics, I find the interconnections between its sub-fields especially enriching. My research combines theoretical and data-driven approaches in physics, spanning cosmology, astrophysics, and geophysics.

Cosmology, as its name suggests, is the study of the Universe as a whole. Due to the finiteness of the speed of light, powerful telescopes today allow cosmologists to decipher the past history of the Universe by looking back in space (which is equivalent to looking back in time!) One of the most exciting periods in the history of the Universe was when the first stars and galaxies were born, known as the epoch of Cosmic Dawn.

Data from upcoming ground- and space-based telescopes will soon allow us an unprecedented view of cosmic dawn via the mapping of hydrogen -- the most abundant element in the Universe. Investigating this era offers valuable clues towards the physics of the normal matter (atoms and molecules) in the Universe and the mechanisms that shaped the formation of the first stars and galaxies.

I developed a novel, data-driven framework which utilises our current astrophysical data to interpret maps of cosmological hydrogen. This approach relied on Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) and Fisher matrix techniques, starting directly from the data to reach meaningful conclusions. I found that this method can easily carry over to interpret cosmological maps of other molecules like carbon monoxide from galaxies, and showed that it can also help extract the properties of the earliest black holes from the observations of the James Webb Space Telescope and gravitational waves.

The methodology used above, at the heart of which is the data itself, finds relevance to some of the most fascinating problems across a variety of subjects today.

Perhaps the most interdisciplinary subject of the present day is Earth Science, which relies on physics, chemistry, biology and more recently, ecology and environmental science. Geology is the study of the earth, while paleontology explores the Earth's past. Today, we are increasingly relying on data and computational tools to decipher Earth's history and relate it to predictions for the future.

Remarkably, many computational and data-driven techniques like the Fisher analysis/MCMC — which are employed heavily in cosmology — also have valuable applications in geophysical and climate science, bringing an important, multi-disciplinary perspective to geophysical research. In the earth sciences, the data driven approach described above is termed as inversion theory. Connecting evidence across widely separated intervals of geological time offers powerful insights that can help us confront contemporary challenges, such as the climate crisis.

During its 4.5 billion-year-old history, our planet has been witness to at least five major extinctions. Of these, the deadliest one was called the Permian Triassic Mass Extinction (PTME), which took place around 252 million years ago and destroyed about 96% of all marine and 70% of all terrestrial species. Intriguingly, it was likely triggered by a global warming runaway, caused by prolonged volcanic activity in what is now Siberia. I used the currently available fossil record data and inversion theory with the iLOSCAR model to show that the temperature rise of about 6-7 degrees that unfolded over ~ 80000 years at the PTME is on track to occur over ~ 200 years at our current warming rates. This indicates that current human activities may be warming the Earth hundreds of times faster than the natural causes of the worst extinction in Earth’s history.

Apart from the data-driven aspects, I have also ventured into a fundamental physics problem during my research! It is known that the current universe (the last 6 billion years of cosmic time) is expanding at an accelerated pace. This expansion is believed to occur due to the so-called cosmological constant. The extremely small value of the cosmological constant is a major puzzle in current cosmology (known as the cosmological constant problem). Resolving the cosmological constant problem is one of the important challenges of present-day cosmology.
