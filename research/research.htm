The following gives an overall view of my research directions. Each bold underlined item takes you to the details of my research activities and publications in that area.  

Astrophysics and Cosmology  

Mapping hydrogen -- the most abundant element in the universe -- at the earliest times, allows access to a tremendously rich cosmological and astrophysical dataset, which will facilitiate the most precise constraints, thus far, on theories of physics. The epoch of Cosmic Dawn, when the first stars and galaxies were born -- often seen as the 'final frontier' of research in observational cosmology today -- is within easy reach of future ground- and space-based facilities. Investigating this era offers valuable clues towards the physics of the normal matter (atoms and molecules) in the universe and offers insights into the formation of the first stars and galaxies.

Intensity mapping (a technique in which large cosmological volumes can be mapped without the need to resolve individual objects) is a promising complementary tool to extract exciting physics and astrophysics from cosmological observations. I developed a novel, data-driven framework which utilises our current astrophysical knowledge to interpret future intensity mapping data of cosmological hydrogen. It readily enables synergies with other line intensity mapping interpretations and more traditional galaxy observations. Extending this approach allows insights into the properties of the earliest black holes, using multi-messenger observations from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and gravitational waves from Pulsar Timing Arrays (PTAs).

The current universe (the last 6 billion years of cosmic time) is characterized by the domination of the cosmological constant, which leads to the late-time accelerating phase of the universe. The extremely small value of the cosmological constant is a major puzzle in current cosmology (known as the cosmological constant problem). Resolving the cosmological constant problem is one of the important challenges of present-day cosmology.

Geophysics, Palaeontology and Ecosystems  

Earth science is an extraordinarily interdisciplinary field, requiring inputs from physics, chemistry, biology and increasingly, ecology and environmental science. At the current juncture, it is particularly important to combine insights from data from a variety of sources, such as carbon dioxide and oxygen proxies, temperature records, and ecosystem populations. Bridging data from disparate intervals in geological time also offers unique insights into the tackling of current challenges, such as the climate crisis.
